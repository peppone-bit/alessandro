Alessandro’s Journey
Seizure-free since March 2022 · Current regimen: Fintepla + Orfiril Long

Alessandro is a boy born in January 2018 in southern Italy. His story started early — his first seizure came at just four and a half months old, twenty minutes after a routine meningitis vaccination. It presented as subtle eye and head movements, easy to miss. Epilepsy was diagnosed that same month, and the years that followed have been defined by two parallel searches: a search for answers, and a search for seizure control.

His family relocated to Hamburg, Germany when he was ten months old, where he came under the care of the neuropaediatric team at UKE Hamburg. What followed was a brutal stretch from late 2020 into early 2021: six status epilepticus episodes in four months, repeated PICU admissions, multiple intubations. Rhinovirus, Enterovirus, Bocavirus — viral illness was the consistent trigger. He pulled through each one.

A seventh breakthrough SE in March 2022 — afebrile, from sleep — finally led to the introduction of Fintepla (fenfluramine). It worked. Alessandro has been seizure-free since. His current regimen of Fintepla 2.4 ml twice daily and Orfiril Long 300 mg twice daily has held steady for over three years now.

Alongside seizure management, Alessandro lives with cognitive impairment (WISC-V Global IQ 58, assessed April 2026), expressive language delay, and dyspraxia. Language is a genuine relative strength — he is bilingual in Italian and German, and his verbal comprehension score sits meaningfully higher than his other indices. He attends a special needs school in Hamburg with full-time individual support. He loves music, responds warmly to structure and familiar routines, and forms strong bonds with the adults around him.

For Researchers & Families
Genetic Profile

Confirmed Variant

SCN1A c.-43638_-141-19167delinsT

A deletion in the 5′UTR / promoter region of SCN1A — heterozygous in Alessandro. ACMG classification: likely pathogenic (PS3_mod, PM2_sup, PM5, PP4). Variant was not previously in ClinVar or HGMD. Overlapping deletions in the same SCN1A promoter region have been published as causal for Dravet syndrome (PMID 20506560).

Why Exome Sequencing Missed It

Alessandro had a comprehensive NGS epilepsy panel and Trio Exome at Meyer Hospital Florence in 2019, returning no pathogenic variants. This is because whole exome sequencing does not cover non-coding regions, including the 5′UTR promoter. The diagnosis required Trio Whole Genome Sequencing to capture the deletion — performed at UKE Hamburg in February 2026, with results in March 2026, seven years after the first seizure.

For families with a Dravet phenotype and a negative exome: push for whole genome sequencing. This is not a failure of prior testing — it reflects a genuine technological limitation that is now overcome.

Inheritance & Mosaicism

Alessandro’s father carries the same SCN1A 5′UTR deletion — but only in approximately 20% of his cells (somatic mosaicism, confirmed by Trio analysis). His father had a milder childhood epilepsy, treatable with valproate, and has been seizure-free since around age 11–14. This case illustrates Dravet’s known intra-familial variability with precision: a ~50% allele load in Alessandro produces full Dravet syndrome; ~20% paternal mosaicism produces a much milder, GEFS+-like phenotype. Neither classical genetic testing nor clinical history would have predicted this inheritance pattern without Trio WGS.

💡 For families in a similar situation: If your child has a Dravet phenotype, you have had a negative exome, and either parent has any personal or family history of febrile seizures or epilepsy — paternal or maternal mosaicism is a real diagnostic possibility. Trio Whole Genome Sequencing is the investigation to request. This family’s case is a concrete example of how it changes the answer.
